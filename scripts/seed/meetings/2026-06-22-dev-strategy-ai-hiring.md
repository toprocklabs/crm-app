## 1. Why MHM wants its own software

*Paul's framing of the impetus and the pain points behind a 3-year investment.*

- **Cost stack.** The firm pays for many separate softwares, each covering one component they need. Tallied up, the total is high enough that building (and even just licensing to themselves) would **pay back in ~3 years** versus continuing to subscribe.
- **Disconnected tools.** They've used **piecemeal case-management software** that doesn't talk to each other — different practices need different systems (Jeff's is specific to his industry and wouldn't work for the others, and vice versa).
- **Efficiency on firm-wide tasks.** Because the tools are siloed, things like **conflict-of-interest checks** are slow and manual. One integrated system would make them near-instant.
- **Bigger vision.** Beyond making a medium-sized firm's life easier, they explicitly want something they could **market and sell** to other firms.

## 4. Top Rock's reframe — build vs. buy

*Austin was transparent that the most honest advice slightly undersells Top Rock's own services.*

Drawing on his prior dev-shop/agency experience (Line Break), Austin laid out **two viable paths** — and flagged that a **custom dev-shop build is the expensive path to avoid** (agencies are incentivized to bill per hour/feature and keep billing).

- **Path A — hire in-house (recommended).** Bring on someone who dives deep and *cares about the firm's needs* far more than any agency will. Best when software is this critical to the business's future.
- **Path B — buy the right all-in-one.** Find the right AI-era, correctly-priced all-in-one tool that actually fits — essentially what they're trying to escape, but viable if the right product exists.

Reference points: Austin is at **Workstream** and his brother is at **Lucid** — both run 50–60-engineer teams focused purely on building; a real custom product needs that kind of dedicated, fast-iterating ownership, which a per-project dev shop can't provide.

## 5. The hiring approach

*The heart of the recommendation: one strong, AI-native engineer, structured as a partner.*

- **Profile:** a strong engineer who **knows AI extremely well**. With the right person, simple prototypes can ship *within a week*.
- **Structure:** start them as a **full-time contractor**, with the goal of converting to **employee → partner with equity**. Skin in the game aligns incentives — they want it to succeed and ship fast, vs. a dev shop billing by the hour.
- **Intensity:** ideally someone **on-site, ~10–12 hours/day**, thinking only about this project — "the best way to build software."
- **Start small.** Don't try to build/replace everything at once. **Nail one thing extremely well, fast**, then iterate over time. (The current product already sprawls — messaging and many features at once.)
- **Two talent paths:** (a) a **hungry new grad** (e.g. BYU) who is AI-native and sharp — ~**$100k/yr**; or (b) a **more experienced** engineer who costs more, offset with **more equity** and a "build this company together" pitch with less cash up front.
- **Timing is favorable.** The engineer market is soft (layoffs) because one strong AI-native engineer now does the work of five — good leverage for hiring.
- **How to find them:** tap the firm's network — ask "who's the best engineer you know?" and follow the referral chain. Referrals are how Top Rock hires.

## 6. Top Rock's role

*Honest about bandwidth: this build is too big for Top Rock to own, but they'll be a high-leverage partner.*

- **Thought partner:** help shape the strategy and de-risk the decision.
- **Prototypes:** Top Rock can build **clickable concepts within weeks** for MHM to play with — and a prototype makes it far easier to recruit ("here's the vision, here's the product").
- **Interview & vet candidates:** because the partners "don't speak the language," Top Rock can sit in on interviews and **technically vet** finalists / narrow to top candidates — explicitly the part MHM was most excited about (their biggest fear is hiring the wrong person again).
- **Not the full build:** Top Rock won't commit the hours a project this size demands; the internal hire takes it from prototype to real software.

## 7. Selling the software (later)

- **Be your own first customer.** The firm already runs the business — build a great product for themselves first, prove it, then sell.
- **Then hire sales / BD.** Once it's proven, bring on business-development people to drive the sell side.
- **Utah talent advantage.** Utah has excellent salespeople (it's why Workstream — a Silicon Valley company — built its sales office here).
- **Poach unhappy competitors.** People unhappy at Filevine would be glad to sell a better product. Legal-world selling is its own different motion.
